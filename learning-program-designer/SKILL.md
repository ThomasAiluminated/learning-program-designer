# Learning Program Designer

Turn a training request into a program that changes what people do at work, not just what they know at the end of the day. The most common failure in L&D is designing content before understanding the problem: a "time management training" for a team whose real issue is unclear priorities from management. This skill starts from the performance need, designs backwards from it, and builds in transfer and evaluation from the start.

## Language

Reply in the language the user writes in and write the program in that language. Use the user's own terms for their context (e.g. nascholing, opleidingstraject, vorming, formation continue, upskilling program).

## Workflow

### 1. Clarify the need (briefly)
Before designing, check the essentials. If they're missing, ask — max 4 questions, one message — because a wrong guess here makes the whole program miss:
- **Who** are the participants (role, experience, number)?
- **What should they do differently** afterwards, on the job? (The behaviour, not the topic.)
- **Why now** — what problem or change triggered the request?
- **Constraints**: total time, format (in person / online / blended), budget, deadline.

Also consider, and say so briefly when relevant, whether training is the right answer at all. If the problem is unclear processes, missing tools or conflicting incentives, a training alone won't fix it; suggest what else is needed alongside it. Clients respect this, and it protects the program's credibility.

If the user just wants a quick outline, make reasonable assumptions, list them, and design.

### 2. Write the objectives
- 3–6 **performance objectives**, each with an observable verb and, where useful, the condition and standard ("After the program, team leads can run a 15-minute feedback conversation using the SBI model, as observed in a role-play").
- Separate **knowledge, skill and attitude** where it helps, but keep the focus on what people will do.
- Every activity in the program must serve at least one objective; content that serves none gets cut. This keeps programs lean.

### 3. Design the learning journey
- **Before**: short preparation that activates prior knowledge or collects real cases from participants (a 10-minute survey, a short video, bring-your-own-case).
- **During**: session-by-session plan. Within each session, follow a rhythm of short input → practice → feedback → reflection. Give most of the time to practice on realistic tasks, not presentation. Vary methods (cases, role-play, peer coaching, simulation, worked examples) and match them to the objective — you learn feedback conversations by having them, not by reading about them.
- **After**: transfer support, which is where most programs fail. Examples: an action plan with a manager check-in, spaced follow-up micro-tasks, a peer-learning session after 4–6 weeks, job aids or checklists.
- Spread practice over time when the format allows; several shorter moments beat one long day for retention.
- Note where **AI tools** can genuinely help (e.g. practice conversations, personalised feedback on assignments, a job aid chatbot), without making them the point.

### 4. Plan evaluation
Use the **Kirkpatrick levels** as a checklist, and keep it proportionate to the program's size:
- **Reaction**: a short, specific feedback form (relevance and confidence to apply, not just satisfaction).
- **Learning**: how you'll know objectives were reached during the program (observed practice, a quiz, a product).
- **Behaviour**: what you'll check after 1–3 months, and who checks it (manager, self, peers).
- **Results**: the business or organisational indicator that should move, if one is realistic. Don't promise ROI the program can't credibly show.

## Output format

1. **Assumptions and need** — 2–4 lines: the performance problem as you understand it, plus assumptions. Include a "training alone won't solve…" note if relevant.
2. **Objectives** — numbered.
3. **Program overview** — one short table: phase (before / sessions / after), format, duration, objectives covered.
4. **Session plans** — per session a table: time, activity, method, objective, materials. Timings must add up to the session length.
5. **Transfer plan** — what happens after, who is responsible.
6. **Evaluation plan** — per Kirkpatrick level: what, how, when.
7. **Next steps** — one or two concrete items (e.g. "validate the objectives with two team leads", "collect three real cases for the role-play").

For larger programs, offer to turn the plan into a document, a slide deck for the client pitch, or a facilitator guide.

## Example (abridged)

**Input:** "Our team leads give hardly any feedback. Design a training, max 1 day."

**Need:** team leads avoid feedback conversations, probably due to lack of skill and confidence. Assumption: there is no formal feedback moment in the current way of working. Training helps with skill and confidence; it also helps if leadership builds a monthly feedback moment into the team rhythm.

**Objective 1:** Team leads can give feedback using the SBI model in a 10-minute conversation, as observed in a role-play with a peer.

**Program:** 2 half days spaced 3 weeks apart (better transfer than 1 full day), with a "give feedback twice" assignment in between and a peer check-in after 6 weeks.
